The Roman Republic and the Rise of Julius Caesar

Julius Caesar was born in 100 BC into the patrician Julian clan. Despite his noble birth, Caesar aligned himself with the populares, a faction that advocated for the common people of Rome. He rose through the Roman political system via the cursus honorum, holding positions including military tribune, quaestor in Hispania, aedile, pontifex maximus, and praetor.

Caesar formed the First Triumvirate in 60 BC with Pompey the Great and Marcus Licinius Crassus. This informal political alliance allowed the three men to dominate Roman politics. Pompey was Rome's greatest general at the time, with victories in the East. Crassus was Rome's wealthiest man, famous for crushing the slave revolt led by Spartacus. Caesar provided political maneuvering and ambition that complemented his partners.

After his consulship in 59 BC, Caesar became governor of Gaul. Over the next decade, the Gallic Wars transformed Caesar into Rome's foremost military commander. He conquered all of Gaul, led two expeditions to Britain, and crossed the Rhine into Germanic territory. His commentaries on the Gallic Wars remain a masterpiece of Latin prose and military history.

The triumvirate collapsed after Crassus was killed at the Battle of Carrhae in 53 BC fighting the Parthians. Pompey and Caesar became rivals. The Roman Senate, aligned with Pompey and the optimates, ordered Caesar to disband his army. Caesar instead crossed the Rubicon river in 49 BC — a legally forbidden act that began a civil war.

After defeating Pompey at the Battle of Pharsalus in 48 BC (Pompey fled to Egypt where he was assassinated), Caesar became master of the Roman world. He was appointed dictator perpetuo — dictator in perpetuity — in 44 BC. This concentration of power alarmed senators who feared the end of the Republic.

Marcus Junius Brutus and Gaius Cassius Longinus led a conspiracy of 60 senators called the Liberatores. On the Ides of March (March 15), 44 BC, they stabbed Caesar 23 times in the Theatre of Pompey. Caesar's death plunged Rome into another round of civil wars.

Mark Antony, Caesar's loyal lieutenant, delivered a funeral oration that turned public opinion against the conspirators. Caesar's adopted son Octavian formed the Second Triumvirate with Antony and Lepidus. They defeated Brutus and Cassius at the Battle of Philippi in 42 BC.

The Second Triumvirate eventually fractured. Octavian and Mark Antony fought for control of Rome. Antony had aligned himself with Cleopatra VII of Egypt, which Octavian used as propaganda to paint Antony as un-Roman. At the Battle of Actium in 31 BC, Octavian's fleet defeated Antony and Cleopatra. Both subsequently died by suicide in Egypt.

Octavian returned to Rome as the undisputed master of the Roman world. In 27 BC, he cleverly arranged to receive the title Augustus from the Senate while maintaining the appearance of republican institutions. This marked the transition from the Roman Republic to the Roman Empire. Augustus Caesar ruled until his death in 14 AD, establishing the Julio-Claudian dynasty.
